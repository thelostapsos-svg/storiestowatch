Every so often a watch appears that the market has never seen — no prior sale, no catalogue history, no auction record to anchor it. In early October 2026, one did: a stainless-steel Rolex Cosmograph Daytona “Paul Newman” Ref. 6241, made around 1968 and, per Sotheby’s, recently rediscovered in a private European collection where it had sat untouched for decades. Offered in the house’s timed online Fine Watches sale in Paris, it was estimated at €120,000–260,000. It hammered at €350,000 and, with buyer’s premium, closed at €448,000 — about $500,700 — after fifteen bids. A watch no one knew existed became, in a few minutes, a half-million-dollar object.

What happened

A ghost from 1968 clears its estimate

No history, no prior sale — and that was the whole appeal.

Sotheby’s described the lot as a steel Ref. 6241 with a black “exotic” dial and contrasting white sub-dials, discovered in a private European collection where it had been kept away from the market for many years (HauteTime). In vintage Rolex, that phrase — fresh to market — is close to magic. A watch that has never circulated carries no doubts about swapped parts, re-dials or restoration-by-committee; collectors pay a premium precisely because there is nothing to disprove.

The bidding reflected it. Against a high estimate of €260,000, the hammer fell at €350,000 — and the all-in price of €448,000 (roughly $500,700) cleared the top estimate by almost €90,000. It took fifteen bids to get there, the signature of a watch several people decided they could not let go.

The backstory

What actually makes a “Paul Newman”

It’s a dial, a nickname, and one of the great accidents in watch history.

“Paul Newman” is not a model — it is a dial. In the 1960s and ’70s, Rolex offered its manual-wind Cosmograph Daytona with an optional “exotic” dial: Art-Deco block numerals on the sub-registers, a stepped outer minute track, and little cross-hair details. It was the slow seller — so unpopular that dealers reportedly swapped the dials out to shift the watches. The ones that survived untouched are now the most coveted dials in all of vintage Rolex.

The nickname came from the man. Actor Paul Newman — racing driver as well as movie star — wore an exotic-dial Ref. 6239 given to him by his wife Joanne Woodward, its caseback engraved “DRIVE CAREFULLY ME.” Italian collectors attached his name to the dial, and it stuck. In 2017, Newman’s own watch sold for $17,752,500 at auction — at the time the most expensive wristwatch ever sold (Sotheby’s). Every exotic-dial Daytona since has traded in that long shadow.

Why it matters

A quiet record for the quiet watches

While the headlines chase million-dollar independents, the blue-chip vintage grail held its line.

This result landed in a loud fortnight — the same week a steel independent watchmaker piece smashed its estimate, and a gem-set modern Rolex set a national record in Australia. Against that noise, a fifty-year-old steel chronograph quietly clearing half a million is its own kind of statement: the vintage Paul Newman Daytona remains one of the most liquid, most trusted stores of value in the entire watch world. It doesn’t need a gimmick. It needs only to be real, original and fresh — and this one was.

It also underlines where the money is still flowing. The modern steel Daytona you cannot buy at retail gets the attention; but the vintage Daytona — finite, unrepeatable, authenticated by decades — is where serious long-term collectors keep parking real capital. What it means for you

Thinking about a vintage Daytona? Read this first

With a watch like this, the money is made or lost on authenticity — not on price.

A half-million-dollar result is a headline, not a shopping list — but the lesson underneath it is usable at any budget. In vintage Rolex, originality is the whole game. A “Paul Newman” with a service dial, a swapped bezel or a polished-away case can be worth a fraction of an untouched one that looks almost identical. The references, the dial variants and the correct parts are a genuine specialism — the difference between the two is often invisible to an untrained eye and worth six figures.

So the route matters more than anything. These watches change hands at the major auction houses and through a small circle of specialist vintage dealers — never on trust alone from an unknown seller. Questions, answered

The quick answers

How much did the Paul Newman Daytona sell for at Sotheby’s?

A 1968 Rolex Daytona “Paul Newman” Ref. 6241, fresh to the market, sold for €448,000 (about $500,700) at Sotheby’s timed online Fine Watches sale in Paris in October 2026. It hammered at €350,000 against an estimate of €120,000–260,000, clearing the high estimate after fifteen bids.

What is a “Paul Newman” Daytona?

“Paul Newman” is not a model but a dial: the Art-Deco “exotic” dial optionally fitted to Rolex’s manual-wind Cosmograph Daytona in the 1960s and 1970s (references 6239, 6241, 6262, 6263, 6264 and 6265). It is named after actor Paul Newman, whose own exotic-dial Ref. 6239 sold for $17,752,500 in 2017.

What is Rolex Daytona reference 6241?

The Ref. 6241 is a steel, manual-wind Cosmograph Daytona made from roughly 1965 to 1969, with a black acrylic tachymetre bezel and pump-style (non-screw-down) pushers. Fitted with the exotic dial, it qualifies as a “Paul Newman” and is one of the classic early references.

How much is a Paul Newman Daytona worth today?

Genuine, original steel examples typically range from roughly $200,000 to $550,000 depending on the reference, dial variant and condition, with the rarest gold and screw-down variants reaching seven figures. Paul Newman’s own watch holds the record at $17,752,500. Any single figure is reference- and condition-specific, so a real valuation matters.
